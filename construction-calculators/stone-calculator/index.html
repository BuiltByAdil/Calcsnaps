Buying stone almost always runs into the same snag: you measure your project in feet, but your supplier sells by the ton. This stone calculator solves that mismatch.

Enter your project's dimensions, area, or a volume you already know. It converts your measurements into cubic yards, a realistic tonnage range, and an estimated cost. It's built for anyone ordering crushed stone, river rock, decorative landscape stone, rip rap, or pea gravel: homeowners planning a driveway base, landscapers pricing a decorative bed, or DIYers topping off a walkway.

Because stone density varies by type and source, the calculator reports weight as a range rather than a single misleadingly precise number, so what you see is closer to what your supplier will actually quote. Use the tool above to get your numbers, then read on for the formula, a worked example, and the details that keep people from over- or under-ordering.

1. How the Stone Calculator Works

The calculator takes three possible starting points: your project's length, width, and depth; a known area plus depth; or a volume you've already calculated. It walks the same three steps regardless of which one you use.

First, it finds the area of your project shape (rectangle, circle, or triangle). Second, it multiplies that area by your chosen depth to get volume, converting inches to feet automatically. Third, it applies the density of your selected stone type, expressed in tons per cubic yard, to convert that volume into a weight.

Real stone density depends on the specific rock, its moisture content, and how tightly it's graded. So the calculator shows that final weight as a range (for example, "4.3 to 5.2 tons") instead of one falsely precise figure. If you add a waste percentage, it's applied to the volume before the weight conversion. If you enter a price, the same range logic carries through to your estimated cost.

Planning-grade values, not a lab result.

A quarry's crushed limestone and a landscape yard's decorative river rock can have meaningfully different densities even within the "crushed stone" category. Treat the result as a strong starting number to confirm with your supplier, not a guaranteed order quantity.

2. How to Use the Stone Calculator

  1. Choose your calculation mode: Dimensions (if you're measuring length, width, and depth yourself), Area (if you already know your square footage), or Volume (if you've already calculated cubic yards or cubic feet).
  2. Pick your shape if you're in Dimensions mode: rectangle for most beds and driveways, circle for round planters or tree rings, triangle for corner lots and odd angles.
  3. Enter your measurements, switching units if needed (inches, feet, yards, centimeters, or meters are all supported).
  4. Set your depth, either by typing a number or tapping a preset: 2 inches for a thin decorative topping, 3 inches for a standard landscape bed, 4 inches for a walkway base, or 6 inches for a driveway base.
  5. Select your stone type from the material dropdown, or choose custom to enter your own density if your supplier gave you one.
  6. Adjust the waste percentage if your site is sloped, irregular, or you want extra buffer. 10% is a reasonable starting point for most flat, regular projects.
  7. Optionally enter a price per ton or per cubic yard to see an estimated cost range.
  8. Read your results: area covered, volume in cubic feet and cubic yards, order volume with waste included, weight in tons, equivalent bags (if your order is small), and estimated cost. Download a PDF summary if you want to bring the numbers to your supplier.

3. Stone Calculator Formula

Volume:

Area (sq ft) = shape-specific formula (see below)
Volume (cu ft) = Area (sq ft) × Depth (ft)
Volume (cu yd) = Volume (cu ft) ÷ 27

Where:

  • Area depends on shape: rectangle is length × width; circle is π × radius²; triangle is (base × height) ÷ 2.
  • Depth (ft): if you entered depth in inches, divide by 12 first.
  • 27 is the number of cubic feet in one cubic yard (3 ft × 3 ft × 3 ft), the standard conversion used throughout U.S. construction and confirmed in NIST's official unit conversion guidance.

Weight:

Order volume (cu yd) = Volume (cu yd) × (1 + waste% ÷ 100)
Weight (tons) = Order volume (cu yd) × density (tons per cu yd)

Where density is material-specific. For example, crushed stone typically runs 1.4 to 1.7 tons per cubic yard, while lighter materials like lava rock can run under 1 ton per cubic yard. Because that range is real, not a rounding error, the calculator carries it through to the final weight instead of picking a single midpoint.

4. Worked Example

Say you're covering a 20 ft × 15 ft rectangular landscape bed with crushed stone at a 3-inch depth, using the default 10% waste allowance.

  1. Area: 20 ft × 15 ft = 300 sq ft
  2. Depth in feet: 3 in ÷ 12 = 0.25 ft
  3. Volume: 300 sq ft × 0.25 ft = 75 cu ft
  4. Cubic yards: 75 ÷ 27 = 2.78 cu yd
  5. Order volume with 10% waste: 2.78 × 1.10 = 3.06 cu yd
  6. Weight (crushed stone density 1.4 to 1.7 tons/yd³): 3.06 × 1.4 = 4.28 tons, and 3.06 × 1.7 = 5.20 tons

So this project needs roughly 4.3 to 5.2 tons of crushed stone, a range you can hand directly to a supplier, who will typically round up to the nearest half or full ton anyway.

5. Understanding Your Result

The tonnage range you get isn't a sign the calculator is unsure of the math. It's an honest reflection of how much real stone density varies by source, moisture, and how tightly it's graded.

When you call a supplier, ask for their specific material's tons-per-yard figure. If they give you a number inside the calculator's range, your order is well-estimated. If their number falls outside it, use theirs, since suppliers know their own product better than any generic planning tool can.

As a rule of thumb, round your final order up, not down. Running short mid-project usually costs more in a second delivery fee than simply ordering a bit extra up front. If your site has significant slope or irregular edges, or you're compacting a base layer, lean toward the higher end of the suggested waste range.

6. Units and Conversions

Suppliers in the U.S. almost always sell by the short ton (2,000 lb). If a supplier quotes in metric tonnes (1,000 kg, about 2,205 lb), the two aren't interchangeable. A metric tonne is roughly 10% heavier than a U.S. short ton, which matters on a large order. If a quote simply says "ton," confirm which one before you place the order.

For volume, 1 cubic yard equals 27 cubic feet (3 ft × 3 ft × 3 ft), the standard conversion this calculator uses throughout, consistent with official U.S. measurement guidance. The calculator also accepts centimeters and meters directly if you're working in metric.

7. Practical Tips

  • Measure at multiple points if your project area isn't perfectly flat or rectangular. An average depth across a few spots gives a more accurate volume than a single measurement.
  • Keep your units consistent while entering dimensions. The calculator converts automatically, but double-check you selected the right unit for each field before reading the result.
  • Ask your supplier for their exact density figure if you have one. Plug it into the custom density option for the most accurate possible weight.
  • Increase your waste allowance on sloped or irregular sites. Flat, simple rectangles need less buffer than curved beds or uneven terrain.
  • Confirm short ton vs. metric tonne before finalizing a large order, since the difference compounds quickly at scale.
  • For compacted base layers (driveways, patio sub-bases), remember that compaction reduces the finished depth of loose material. Many contractors order 20 to 25% more loose volume than the finished compacted depth would suggest, on top of ordinary waste allowance.

8. Common Mistakes

  • Confusing square feet with cubic feet. Square footage only measures area. You still need depth to get a real volume, and skipping that step is the single most common estimating error.
  • Mixing inches and feet without converting. Entering a depth of "3" without specifying inches vs. feet produces a result that's off by a factor of 12. Always double-check the unit shown next to the field.
  • Using one density figure for every stone type. Crushed stone, river rock, and lightweight decorative stone can differ by 30% or more in tons per cubic yard. Picking the wrong material preset overstates or understates your order significantly.
  • Ignoring compaction for base layers. A 4-inch compacted driveway base needs more than 4 inches of loose material before it's rolled or tamped down. Ordering exactly the finished depth in loose volume leaves you short.
  • Rounding too early in the calculation. Round only your final order quantity, not intermediate steps like cubic feet or cubic yards. Early rounding compounds into a meaningfully wrong final tonnage on larger projects.
