Bitumen Calculator Guide: How to Calculate Bitumen Quantity for a Road

From road length and layer thickness to tonnes of bitumen, with density conversion, binder percentage, and prime and tack coat worked out step by step

A bitumen calculator estimates how much bitumen binder a road or pavement layer needs, starting from its length, width, thickness, mix density, and binder percentage. It is useful for contractors, site engineers, quantity surveyors, students, and anyone pricing a driveway or car park, because bitumen is bought by weight, and an estimate that is a few tonnes out can mean a delayed delivery or an expensive surplus.

This bitumen calculator handles several pavement layers at once and returns the bitumen in tonnes, kilograms, and litres, along with mix weight, aggregate weight, drums, and an optional cost. Two more tabs cover prime and tack coat quantities and m³-to-tonnes conversion. Enter your measurements in the calculator above to see every step of the working.

Quick answer:

Bitumen (kg) = Length (m) × Width (m) × Thickness (m) × Mix density (kg/m³) × Binder % ÷ 100. A 1,000 m road, 7 m wide, with a 50 mm layer at 2,350 kg/m³ and 5.5% binder holds 350 m³ of mix, which weighs 822.5 tonnes, and 5.5% of that is about 45.2 tonnes of bitumen. Work in metres and compacted values, then add a small allowance for waste.

1. How the Bitumen Calculator Works

The Road / Pavement Mix tab describes the road once and then each layer separately. You enter the road length and width, and for each layer its compacted thickness, the compacted mix density, and the binder content. The calculator multiplies length by width to get the area, multiplies the area by each layer's thickness to get volume, and converts that volume to weight with the mix density.

It then takes the binder percentage of each layer's weight to find the bitumen, and treats the remainder as aggregate, which includes filler. Layers are added together, so a wearing course over a binder course over a base course is one calculation instead of three separate ones. The waste allowance is applied to the total bitumen to give the quantity to order, and the net figure is always shown beside it.

From the net weight and the bitumen density you enter, the calculator also reports the bitumen volume in litres, and from your drum size it counts the drums, rounded up. If you give a price, it multiplies the order quantity by it.

Assumptions:

The road is treated as a rectangle of constant width, and each layer as a uniform slab at its finished, compacted thickness. Binder content is taken as a percentage of the total mix weight, which is the usual way mix designs state it. The calculator does not add allowance for tapered edges, camber, shoulders, patching, or thickness that varies over an uneven base, so measure irregular areas in sections and enter them one at a time. Prime coat and tack coat are separate sprayed applications and are not part of the mix, which is why they have their own tab.

2. How to Use the Bitumen Calculator

  1. Choose Road / Pavement Mix, and pick metric or imperial units.
  2. Enter the road length and width. For a 1 km road, enter 1000 m, and the result also shows bitumen per kilometre.
  3. For each layer, choose a mix type to fill typical values, or leave it on Custom, then enter the thickness, mix density, and binder percentage from your specification.
  4. Use Add Another Layer for each additional course, up to 4 layers.
  5. Check the bitumen density, set the waste allowance, and enter the drum size if you order in drums.
  6. Optionally enter a price per tonne for bitumen (charged on the order quantity) and for asphalt mix (charged on the net mix weight) to see an estimated cost.
  7. Read the bitumen to order, the layer-by-layer table, and the step-by-step working, then use Copy, Share, or Download PDF to keep a record.

For prime or tack coat, switch to the Prime / Tack Coat tab and enter the area and spray rate. For converting m³ to tonnes or finding a binder percentage, use the Convert & Percent tab. If a value is outside the allowed range the calculator says so rather than changing it silently. Most fields use the nearest allowed value, while a bitumen density that is empty or outside its accepted range is replaced by the 1,020 kg/m³ default. It also flags values that look unusual, such as a binder content far outside the normal range.

3. Bitumen Calculation Formula

Mix volume (m³) = Length (m) × Width (m) × Thickness (m) Mix weight (kg) = Mix volume (m³) × Mix density (kg/m³) Bitumen (kg) = Mix weight (kg) × Binder % ÷ 100

Thickness must be in metres, so divide millimetres by 1000 first: 50 mm is 0.05 m. Divide kilograms by 1000 for tonnes.

Combined, the three steps give one line you can check by hand, and the sum of all layers is the project total:

Bitumen (kg) = L × W × T × ρ × B ÷ 100 Aggregate (kg) = Mix weight − Bitumen Bitumen to order = Net bitumen × (1 + Waste %)

L = length, W = width, T = compacted thickness, ρ = compacted mix density, and B = binder content as a percentage of total mix weight.

Two derived figures are handy for checking a result. Bitumen per square metre is the bitumen weight divided by the area, and bitumen volume in litres is the bitumen weight in kilograms divided by its density in kg/m³, multiplied by 1000. For a prime or tack coat there is no mix, so the formula is simply area × spray rate × number of coats.

4. Worked Example: 1 km, Two Layers

These are the calculator's default values: a road 1,000 m long and 7 m wide with a 40 mm wearing course at 2,350 kg/m³ and 5.5% binder, over a 60 mm binder course at 2,400 kg/m³ and 4.5% binder. The bitumen density is 1,020 kg/m³, the waste allowance is 3%, and drums hold 200 kg.

LayerVolumeMix WeightBinderBitumen
Wearing course (40 mm)280 m³658.00 t5.5%36.19 t
Binder course (60 mm)420 m³1,008.00 t4.5%45.36 t
Total700 m³1,666.00 t4.9%81.55 t

The area is 1,000 × 7 = 7,000 m². The wearing course volume is 7,000 × 0.040 = 280 m³, which at 2,350 kg/m³ weighs 658,000 kg, and 5.5% of that is 36,190 kg of bitumen. The binder course volume is 7,000 × 0.060 = 420 m³, which weighs 1,008,000 kg, and 4.5% of that is 45,360 kg.

Together the layers need 81,550 kg, or 81.55 tonnes, of bitumen in 1,666 tonnes of mix, which leaves 1,584.45 tonnes of aggregate. The overall binder content works out to 81.55 ÷ 1,666 = 4.9%. The spread is 81,550 ÷ 7,000 = 11.65 kg per square metre, and since the road is exactly 1 km long, the same 81.55 tonnes is the figure per kilometre.

A 3% allowance for waste gives 81.55 × 1.03 = 84.00 tonnes to order. In litres, 81,550 ÷ 1,020 × 1000 = 79,951 litres, or about 80 m³ of bitumen. With 200 kg drums, 83,996.5 kg ÷ 200 = 419.98, which rounds up to 420 drums.

5. Understanding Your Result

The headline figure is the bitumen to order, which is the net requirement plus your waste allowance. Take that to your supplier, and use the net figure when you compare against a bill of quantities or a mix design, because those documents rarely include wastage.

This is an estimate, not a measurement. It is exact for the inputs you give it, but real quantities depend on how closely the finished thickness matches the design, how well the mix is compacted, and how much binder the plant actually adds. A few millimetres of extra thickness over a long road adds tonnes of mix, so the result is only as good as the thickness and density you start with.

For rounding, keep full precision through the calculation and round only at the end. Bitumen is normally quoted in tonnes to two decimals, and drums or tanker loads are rounded up to whole units, because you cannot order a fraction of a drum. If the result sits just above a whole tanker load, check whether a small reduction in waste, or a rounding choice on the length, is hiding a real shortfall.

6. Bitumen Content in Asphalt

Binder content is the share of the mix that is bitumen. Most asphalt mixes for roads contain somewhere between about 4% and 7% bitumen by weight, and the exact figure comes from a laboratory mix design for the aggregate and traffic. Different layers use different amounts, so the typical values below are starting points, not substitutes for your specification.

Mix typeTypical binder (% of mix)Typical compacted densityWhere it is used
Dense-graded wearing course5.0 to 6.0%2,300 to 2,420 kg/m³Top layer under traffic
Binder or intermediate course4.0 to 5.0%2,350 to 2,450 kg/m³Spreads load below the surface
Base course3.5 to 4.5%2,350 to 2,450 kg/m³Structural lower layer
Stone mastic asphalt6.0 to 7.0%2,300 to 2,400 kg/m³Heavy traffic, rut resistance

To find the percentage from weights, divide the weight of bitumen by the total weight of the mix and multiply by 100. If a plant used 27.5 tonnes of bitumen in 500 tonnes of mix, the binder content is 5.5%.

One detail catches people out: some specifications state binder as a percentage of the aggregate weight rather than of the total mix. The two are not the same number. 5.5% by weight of the mix equals 5.5 ÷ 94.5 = 5.82% by weight of the aggregate, and the conversion in the other direction is aggregate % ÷ (100 + aggregate %). The Convert & Percent tab does this for you, and this calculator always expects the percentage of total mix. The Asphalt Institute's engineering glossary treats bitumen and asphalt binder as the same material, which is why "bitumen" and "asphalt binder" are used interchangeably here.

7. Bitumen Density and m³ to Tonnes

Paving bitumen is slightly denser than water. A typical planning figure is about 1,020 kg/m³, and common grades fall roughly between 1,000 and 1,050 kg/m³, so one cubic metre weighs a little over one tonne. Always prefer the value on your supplier's data sheet, together with the temperature it applies to.

Volume to weight is a multiplication, and weight to volume is a division:

Tonnes = m³ × Density (kg/m³) ÷ 1000 m³ = Tonnes × 1000 ÷ Density (kg/m³)

At 1,020 kg/m³, 10 m³ weighs 10.2 tonnes, and 10 tonnes occupies about 9.8 m³, or 9,804 litres.

Density falls as bitumen is heated, so a cubic metre of hot bitumen in a tank weighs less than a cubic metre at ambient temperature. That is why bitumen is normally bought and invoiced by weight, and why a tank's volume reading is only a rough guide to the tonnes inside. Use weight for ordering, and use volume only for storage and pumping capacity.

8. Prime Coat and Tack Coat

A prime coat is sprayed on an unbound granular base before paving, and a tack coat is sprayed between asphalt layers so they bond. Neither is part of the mix, so their bitumen is calculated separately, from the area and a spray rate:

Quantity (kg) = Area (m²) × Spray rate (kg/m²) × Number of coats

Litres = kg ÷ density (kg/m³) × 1000. In US units, enter the rate in gallons per square yard.

Spray rates vary with the product and the surface. Prime coat rates on granular bases are commonly under 1.2 kg/m², and tack coat rates are much lower, often a few tenths of a kilogram per square metre, but your specification and the supplier's data sheet decide. Check whether the rate refers to the emulsion or cutback you actually spray or to the residual bitumen left after the water or solvent has gone, because an emulsion contains water and the two figures can differ a lot. The tab lets you choose either basis, and for a residual rate you enter the residual bitumen content of your product, for example the percentage on its data sheet. The presets are only starting points.

9. Units and Terminology

  • Bitumen: the black, sticky binder in asphalt. In North America it is usually called asphalt binder or asphalt cement.
  • Asphalt or asphalt mix: bitumen combined with aggregate and filler. Elsewhere it may be called bituminous concrete or hot mix asphalt.
  • Binder content: bitumen as a percentage of the total mix weight, typically 4% to 7%.
  • Compacted thickness and density: the finished layer after rolling. Use these, not the loose-laid values.
  • Tonne and US ton: a metric tonne is 1,000 kg, and a US (short) ton is 2,000 lb, about 907.2 kg. The imperial setting reports US tons.
  • Useful conversions: 1 ft = 0.3048 m, 1 in = 25.4 mm, 1 lb/ft³ = 16.018 kg/m³, 1 US gallon = 3.785 litres, 1 yd² = 0.8361 m².

Mix density can also be given as bulk specific gravity. Multiply that by about 1,000 to get kg/m³, so a bulk specific gravity of 2.35 corresponds to roughly 2,350 kg/m³.

10. Practical Tips

  • Use the finished, compacted thickness from the design. Loose mix leaves the paver thicker than the finished layer, so loose depth overstates the quantity.
  • Take mix density and binder content from the job mix formula or mix design when you have it, and fall back on the typical values only for early estimates.
  • For a road that changes width, split it into sections with a constant width and add the results, or divide the total area by the length to get an average width.
  • Keep the unit system consistent. Switching to imperial converts your existing numbers, but check the labels before you read a result.
  • Enter the drum weight your supplier actually delivers. A drum is often quoted around 200 kg, but net weights vary, and a few kilograms per drum adds up over hundreds of drums.
  • Use the waste allowance for real losses such as tank heels, hose and plant start-up, and keep it separate from any contingency in your contract.
  • If you like a spreadsheet, the same three formulas fit in three cells, and the PDF report gives you a printable record of inputs and results without one.
  • Confirm the final order with your bitumen supplier, because grade, delivery temperature, and tanker size affect what can be delivered.

11. Common Mistakes

  • Leaving thickness in millimetres: using 50 instead of 0.05 m makes the answer 1,000 times too large. The calculator converts for you, but a hand check must too.
  • Using the loose-laid thickness: this overstates the quantity by roughly the amount the mix compacts.
  • Treating binder percentage as an addition to the aggregate: 5% of the mix is not 5 kg per 100 kg of aggregate. Check which basis your specification uses.
  • Mixing up bitumen weight and mix weight: a road needs hundreds of tonnes of mix but only a few tens of tonnes of bitumen. Ordering the mix figure as bitumen is a very expensive error.
  • Forgetting that layers differ: applying one binder content and density to every layer misses that wearing courses carry more binder than base courses.
  • Ignoring prime and tack coat: they are extra bitumen outside the mix and are easy to leave out of the order.
  • Treating one litre as one kilogram: bitumen is about 2% heavier than water, so a litre weighs roughly 1.02 kg, which is a real error on a large order.
  • Rounding early: rounding the volume or the percentage before multiplying compounds across a long road.

Conclusion

A road's bitumen quantity comes down to one chain: area times thickness gives volume, volume times mix density gives weight, and weight times binder percentage gives bitumen. This bitumen calculator runs that chain for every layer, adds your waste allowance, and also converts to litres and drums, handles prime and tack coat, and converts m³ to tonnes. Accurate thickness, density, and binder content matter most, because each one multiplies through the whole result. Use the calculator above for your estimate, then confirm the order against your mix design and supplier.
