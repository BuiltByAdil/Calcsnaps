Building with concrete block almost always comes down to one question: how many blocks does this actual wall need, openings included? This concrete block calculator answers that directly. Enter your wall's length and height, subtract any doors or windows, pick a block size, and it returns an estimated block count, mortar bags, sand, an optional grout fill volume, and a cost estimate.

It's built for anyone laying out a CMU, cinder, or cement block wall: homeowners planning a garden or boundary wall, DIYers building a small foundation or utility wall, and contractors who want a fast second check on a takeoff. Use the tool above to get your numbers, then read on for the formula, a worked example, and the details that keep people from over- or under-ordering.

1. How the Concrete Block Calculator Works

The calculator starts with your wall's gross area (length times height), then subtracts any door or window openings you add to get the net area that actually needs block. That net area is divided by the face area of a single block to get an estimated block count.

From there, it applies your waste percentage to get a real order quantity, and uses that final block count to estimate mortar bags and sand. If you're using hollow blocks and choose to fill the cells, it also estimates a grout volume based on your block's thickness and typical hollow-core proportions.

Planning-grade values, not a lab result.

Mortar-bag yield, sand ratios, and hollow-core void percentages vary by manufacturer and mix. Treat the result as a strong starting order to confirm with your supplier, not a guaranteed final quantity.

2. How to Use the Concrete Block Calculator

  1. Enter your wall length and height, switching units if needed (inches, feet, yards, centimeters, or meters are all supported).
  2. Add any openings: click "Add an opening" for each door or window and enter its width and height. Skip this if your wall has none.
  3. Pick a block face size from the standard presets, or choose custom and specify whether your measurement is nominal (includes the mortar joint) or the actual physical block size.
  4. Select the block thickness (4" to 12") and whether the block is hollow or solid.
  5. Check "Fill all cells with grout" if you're grouting a hollow-block wall and want a fill volume estimate.
  6. Adjust the waste percentage based on how many openings and corners your wall has. 5-7% suits a simple wall; complex layouts need more.
  7. Optionally enter a price per block and per mortar bag to see an estimated cost.
  8. Read your results: net wall area, blocks needed before and after waste, mortar bags, sand, grout fill if selected, and cost. Download a PDF summary if you want to bring the numbers to your supplier.

3. Concrete Block Calculator Formula

Wall area and block count:

Gross wall area (sq ft) = Wall length (ft) × Wall height (ft)
Net wall area (sq ft) = Gross wall area − sum of opening areas
Block face area (sq ft) = Block length (in) × Block height (in) ÷ 144
Blocks needed = Net wall area ÷ Block face area
Total blocks to buy = Blocks needed × (1 + waste% ÷ 100)

Where block length and height are nominal dimensions, meaning they already include the mortar joint. For a standard 16" x 8" block, face area is 16 × 8 ÷ 144 = 0.89 sq ft, so a 100 sq ft wall needs about 113 blocks before waste.

Mortar and sand:

Mortar bags = Total blocks ÷ 33.3
Sand (cu yd) = Mortar bags ÷ 7

This follows the common estimating rule of three standard mortar bags per 100 blocks, with roughly one cubic yard of sand for every seven bags if mixing mortar from scratch instead of buying it pre-mixed.

4. Worked Example

Say you're building a 20 ft long, 8 ft high garden wall using standard 16" x 8" blocks, with one 3 ft x 7 ft door opening, using the default 7% waste allowance.

  1. Gross area: 20 ft × 8 ft = 160 sq ft
  2. Opening area: 3 ft × 7 ft = 21 sq ft
  3. Net area: 160 − 21 = 139 sq ft
  4. Block face area: 16 × 8 ÷ 144 = 0.89 sq ft
  5. Blocks needed: 139 ÷ (128 ÷ 144) = 139 ÷ 0.8889 = 156.4, rounds up to 157 blocks
  6. Total blocks to buy with 7% waste: 157 × 1.07 = 168.0, rounds up to 168 blocks
  7. Mortar bags: 168 ÷ 33.3 = 5.04, rounds up to 6 bags

So this wall needs roughly 168 blocks and 6 bags of mortar, a number you can hand directly to a block yard.

5. Understanding Your Result

The block count is rounded up in two places: once for the exact blocks needed, and again after waste is applied. That's intentional. Suppliers sell whole blocks and whole mortar bags, and rounding down at any step risks a second trip to the yard mid-project.

If your net area came out lower than expected, double-check that your openings were entered in the same unit as your wall dimensions. If it came out higher, confirm you didn't accidentally enter a block's actual size where the calculator expected a nominal size, since actual sizes are smaller and produce a higher block count for the same wall.

6. Nominal vs Actual Block Sizes

Every standard block size you'll see, including "16x8" and "8x8," is a nominal size. It already includes a standard 3/8" mortar joint, so the physical block itself is smaller. Standard sizes follow ASTM C90, the industry specification most concrete block manufacturers build to; the Concrete Masonry & Hardscapes Association publishes accessible guidance summarizing these dimensions.

A block sold as 16" x 8" nominal actually measures about 15 5/8" x 7 5/8". Once laid with a 3/8" mortar joint on each side, it occupies the full 16" x 8" of wall space. This is why the calculator's presets use nominal sizes directly, and why a custom entry needs to specify which kind of size you're measuring.

7. Practical Tips

  • Measure openings precisely, including any rough framing, not just the finished door or window size, since the block wall needs to accommodate the full opening.
  • Keep wall and opening units consistent. The calculator converts automatically, but double-check the unit shown next to each field.
  • Raise your waste percentage for complex walls. More corners, more openings, and more cutting all mean more waste.
  • Confirm your block's actual dimensions with your supplier if you're using a non-standard or regional block, since nominal sizes can vary slightly by manufacturer.
  • Decide on grout fill early if your wall is structural or load-bearing, since it affects both material cost and whether steel reinforcement is needed.

8. Common Mistakes

  • Forgetting to deduct openings. A wall with a garage door or several windows can have meaningfully less net area than its gross size suggests.
  • Mixing nominal and actual sizes. Using a block's actual physical size where a nominal size is expected inflates the block count.
  • Applying brick coverage rates to block. Concrete blocks are much larger per unit than bricks; a brick-based coverage rate massively overstates how many blocks a wall needs.
  • Underestimating mortar for larger joints. A thicker-than-standard mortar joint uses more material per block than the 33.3-blocks-per-bag rule assumes.
  • Skipping the waste allowance on complex walls. Corners, openings, and cut blocks all add up faster than a flat rectangle would suggest.

9. Related Calculations and Use Cases

A block wall's footing or base is commonly a compacted gravel layer, which has very different volume and weight needs than the blocks themselves. If you're planning that base separately, our Gravel Calculator can help estimate that material on its own.

Common real-world use cases for this calculator include garden and boundary walls (typically 4"-6" thick blocks), foundation and basement walls (8" or thicker, often filled and reinforced), garage walls with a vehicle door opening, and interior partition walls (usually 4" thick, hollow, unfilled). Each use case generally points to a different thickness and fill decision, which is why those are kept as separate inputs above.
