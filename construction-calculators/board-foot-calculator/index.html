1. What Is a Board Foot?

A board foot is a unit of volume used to buy and sell lumber. One board foot is the amount of wood in a board 1 foot long, 1 foot wide, and 1 inch thick, which is 144 cubic inches or one twelfth of a cubic foot. The unit is written BF, BDFT, or FBM (foot, board measure), and 1,000 board feet is written MBF.

It exists because boards differ in thickness and width, so length alone does not tell you how much wood you are buying. A board 1 in thick, 4 in wide, and 8 ft long holds the same wood as one that is 1 in thick, 8 in wide, and 4 ft long, and the board foot shows that they are equal. Hardwood, rough-sawn lumber, and slabs are usually priced this way, while framing lumber is mostly sold by the piece.

4. Board Foot Formula and How to Calculate It

There are two forms of the formula. They give the same answer and differ only in the unit of length.

Board feet = thickness (in) x width (in) x length (ft) / 12 Board feet = thickness (in) x width (in) x length (in) / 144

Multiply by the number of boards to get the total for a quantity.

To measure board feet by hand, follow these steps.

  1. Measure the thickness of the board in inches. For hardwood, use its quarter designation, such as 4/4 for 1 inch.
  2. Measure the width across the face in inches. For boards with uneven edges, measure at the narrow point or take an average.
  3. Measure the length in feet.
  4. Multiply the three numbers and divide by 12.
  5. For a stack of different boards, repeat for each size and add the results.

To estimate board feet before you buy, work out the size of every part in your plan, calculate each part, add them up, and then add an allowance for waste.

5. Nominal, Rough, and Surfaced Sizes

This is where most mistakes happen. Lumber has a size it is sold as and a size you can measure, and board feet usually use the first one.

Hardwood. Thickness is given in quarters of an inch, a system set by the National Hardwood Lumber Association (NHLA). The quarter number refers to the rough thickness. When a board is planed smooth, it gets thinner, but it is still counted at its rough thickness. A 4/4 board is counted as 1 inch even when it is surfaced to about 13/16 inch.

If you buy surfaced hardwood and calculate with its measured thickness instead of its quarter size, you will come out about 19 percent low on 4/4 stock (13/16 is 0.8125, compared with 1). Rough boards can also vary a little from their nominal thickness, so measure if it matters.

Softwood framing. A 2 x 4 is called that because of its nominal size before drying and planing. It actually measures about 1.5 by 3.5 inches. In the softwood trade, board feet are normally figured from the nominal width and thickness and the actual length, so a 2 x 4 x 8 counts as 2 x 4 x 8 / 12 = 5.33 board feet, not the 3.5 board feet of wood in the piece. Because of this, the quick sizes for dimensional lumber use nominal values.

The practical rule is to use the size the seller uses for pricing. If you are checking how much real wood a board holds, measure it and enter the actual numbers.

8. Board Feet to Square Feet

A board foot is volume and a square foot is area, so you cannot convert them without a thickness. Once you know the thickness, the relationship is simple, because one board foot of 1 inch stock covers one square foot.

Square feet = board feet / thickness (in) Board feet = square feet x thickness (in)

For example, 60 board feet of 3/4 inch stock covers 60 / 0.75 = 80 square feet, and 100 square feet of 3/4 inch stock is 100 x 0.75 = 75 board feet. The Convert tab does this for any thickness, in square feet or square meters. If you only need the area of a room or a surface, our Square Footage Calculator gives it for any mix of shapes.

9. Board Feet to Cubic Feet and Other Units

Because a board foot is a volume, it converts directly to other volume units. One board foot is 144 cubic inches, which is one twelfth of a cubic foot, about 2.36 liters, or 0.00236 cubic meters. Going the other way, one cubic foot is 12 board feet and one cubic meter is about 423.8 board feet. One MBF is 1,000 board feet, or about 83.3 cubic feet.

These conversions use the full volume of the wood as if it were solid. They do not tell you how much lumber a log will yield, which depends on the log rule, sawing losses, and the mill.

10. How to Calculate Board Feet in a Log

Logs are scaled with a log rule, which is a formula or table that estimates the board feet of lumber a log can produce. Three rules are in wide use in the United States, according to Purdue Extension's guide to log and tree scaling. For all of them, D is the diameter at the small end inside the bark in inches, and L is the length in feet.

RuleFormulaNotes
Doyle(D − 4)² × L / 16Simple and common in the east and south. Ignores taper and seriously under-scales small logs.
Scribner(0.79D² − 2D − 4) × L / 16A formula that approximates the diagram-based Scribner rule. Fairly consistent for logs 16 ft or shorter and under 28 in.
International 1/4 in0.04976191LD² + 0.006220239L²D − 0.1854762LD + 0.0002591767L³ − 0.01159226L² + 0.04222222LAllows for taper. Probably the most accurate of the three at estimating actual lumber.

Take a log 16 inches across the small end and 16 feet long. Doyle gives (16 − 4)² × 16 / 16 = 144 board feet. The Scribner formula gives about 166, and the International rule gives about 181. For a smaller log, 10 inches across and 12 feet long, Doyle gives 27 while International gives about 46, so Doyle is far lower on small logs. The Log tab shows all three and how far each one is from the International result.

Measure the diameter at the small end, inside the bark, and average two measurements taken at right angles if the log is not round. Measure the length with a tape, and remember that a log just short of a full foot is normally scaled at the next lower foot. Always agree on a log rule before buying or selling logs. The calculator accepts logs from 6 to 60 inches across and 4 to 20 feet long. Longer logs are normally scaled as two or more shorter ones.

11. Board Foot vs Linear Foot vs Square Foot

  • Linear foot: length only. A 10 ft board is 10 linear feet, whatever its width or thickness.
  • Square foot: area, which is length times width. It ignores thickness.
  • Board foot: volume, which is length times width times thickness, standardized to a 1 ft by 1 ft by 1 in unit.

Two boards with the same length can have very different board feet, and a board's price per board foot lets you compare them fairly.

12. Waste, Tally, and Cost

The exact board feet of your cut list is not what you should buy. Boards have defects, grain and color need matching, and every cut loses wood. Many woodworkers add roughly 15 to 30 percent to a hardwood cut list, and less for straight framing lumber. These are planning figures, so ask your supplier. The waste field in the calculator adds the percentage and shows the board feet to buy.

Dealers also tally boards in their own way. Many tally lengths to the whole foot and drop any fraction, so a board 10 ft 7 in may count as 10 ft, and they round thickness to quarters. Ask how your dealer tallies, and compare your own calculation with the tag when you pick up the lumber.

To estimate cost, multiply the board feet to buy by the price per board foot. Prices change by species, grade, thickness, and whether the board is rough or surfaced, so use your supplier's current price.

13. Common Mistakes

  • Dividing by 12 when the length is in inches. Use 144 for inches and 12 for feet.
  • Using surfaced thickness for hardwood. Use the quarter size, such as 4/4, not the measured 13/16 inch.
  • Mixing nominal and actual sizes in the same list.
  • Forgetting the quantity and counting a stack of boards as one.
  • Treating a board foot like a square foot or a linear foot.
  • Rounding too early, which can add up over a long list.
  • Mixing log rules, or comparing one rule's scale with another's price.
  • Buying the exact number. Add an allowance for defects and cuts.

14. Related Calculations and Use Cases

Board feet are used for furniture, cabinetry, flooring stock, slabs, and timber sales, and the same lumber math shows up in framing. If you are planning wall framing and need to know how many pieces to buy, our Stud Calculator counts studs and plates, and you can then price them by the piece or by the board foot.

For spray foam insulation, the term board foot also means a volume of 1 square foot at 1 inch thick, so the same area-times-thickness idea applies, but check the product's coverage information.
