This wallpaper calculator tells you how many rolls to buy for a room, and it handles pattern repeat, which most quick estimates ignore. Enter each wall, choose or type your roll size, pick the pattern match, and it lays the strips along the roll the way a decorator would. You get the rolls to buy, the strips, the cut length, and an optional cost. It works in inches, feet, yards, centimeters, and meters, so it also serves as a wallpaper estimator, a wallpaper roll calculator, and a wallcovering calculator.

Below you will find the method, measuring steps, an explanation of pattern repeat and match, common roll sizes, worked examples, and why a square footage estimate can leave you short.

Quick answer:

Wallpaper is bought by the strip, not by the square foot. Strips = wall width ÷ roll width, rounded up. Each strip is the wall height plus trim, rounded up to the pattern repeat. Rolls = strips ÷ strips per roll, rounded up.

1. How the Wallpaper Calculator Works

A roll of wallpaper is a long, narrow band. A wall is covered by hanging vertical strips side by side, and each strip must be cut longer than the wall is high, to allow for trimming at the ceiling and the floor. The calculator follows the same steps you would use by hand.

First it divides each wall's width by the roll width and rounds up, because a part strip still uses a full strip of paper. Next it works out the length of each strip, which is the wall height plus your trim allowance. For a patterned paper, it then places the strips along the roll so that the pattern lines up. In a straight match every strip starts on a repeat boundary. In a half-drop match every other strip starts half a repeat later. Finally it counts how many strips fit on one roll, divides, and rounds up. A spare allowance adds extra strips for mistakes.

Besides the rolls to buy, the results show the rolls needed without spare, the number of strips, strips per roll, the cut length, the paper you will buy, the wall area covered, the share of paper left unused, and a quick area-based estimate for comparison. A breakdown table lists every wall, and a step-by-step block shows the math.

Assumptions and limits:

The calculator assumes standard vertical hanging, even walls, and rolls whose pattern starts at the same point on every roll. It does not handle murals, panels, borders, or walls that slope. Pattern matching around corners can add waste that no calculator can predict. Treat the result as a planning estimate and confirm it with your supplier or installer.

2. How to Use the Wallpaper Calculator

  1. Choose imperial units (feet and inches) or metric units (meters and centimeters).
  2. Enter each wall's width and height. For a plain rectangular room, use Quick Room to create four walls from the length, width, and height.
  3. Pick your roll size from the list, or type the width and length from the label. In imperial mode, roll length can be in feet or yards.
  4. Choose the pattern match and enter the pattern repeat from the label. For a free (random) match, no repeat is needed.
  5. Check the trim allowance, which is the extra length added to every strip for trimming at the ceiling and the floor.
  6. Set the spare allowance. It defaults to 10 percent.
  7. Leave doors and windows on Hang across, or choose Deduct and enter each opening to see an estimate.
  8. Optionally enter the price per roll, then read the rolls to buy, the strips, the breakdown, and the step-by-step math.
  9. Use Copy Result, Share Result, or Download PDF to keep the numbers, and Reset All to start again.

If a measurement is missing, a fraction cannot be read, or a strip is longer than the roll, the calculator tells you which input needs attention instead of showing a number.

3. How to Calculate Wallpaper Needed

Strips per wall = wall width / roll width, rounded up Strip length = wall height + trim Strip step (straight match) = strip length rounded up to a multiple of the repeat Strips per roll = rounded-down ((roll length - strip length) / step) + 1 Rolls = total strips / strips per roll, rounded up

These formulas describe a straight match with walls of one height. The calculator does not rely on a single formula: it lays the strips along the rolls, keeping the pattern aligned, so half-drop matches, walls of different heights, and short pieces are handled by the layout itself. For straight and free matches, a shorter strip can use leftover length at the end of an earlier roll. For a half-drop it keeps the strips in the order you would hang them, which is the more cautious choice. In those cases the number of strips can vary from roll to roll.

The strips-per-roll line reflects that the last strip on a roll only needs its own length, not the rounded-up step, so it can sit right at the end of the roll. A simpler hand method adds one full repeat to the strip length and divides the roll length by that, which is slightly more cautious, and the spare allowance covers the difference.

Part strips count as whole strips. Wallpaper should not be turned fully into a corner, because walls are rarely straight and the paper can crease, so the part-width left over at a corner is calculated as a full strip, as James Dunlop's wallpaper calculation guide explains. That guide also adds trim to each strip and rounds the roll count up with extra for pattern waste, which is the same sequence the calculator follows.

4. How to Measure a Room for Wallpaper

  1. Measure each wall separately. Measure from corner to corner at the widest point. Do not add the walls together, because every wall rounds up to whole strips.
  2. Measure the height of the area you will paper. Go from the ceiling, or the coving, down to the top of the baseboard, or to the floor if you will paper that far.
  3. Use the largest measurement. Walls and ceilings are rarely perfectly level, so measure the height in a few places and use the tallest.
  4. Note every opening. You only need sizes if you plan to deduct them, but it helps to write them down.
  5. Read the label. Find the roll width, roll length, pattern repeat, and match type on the label or product page.

5. Pattern Repeat and Pattern Match

These two terms are often mixed up. The pattern repeat is the vertical distance before the design repeats. The pattern match describes how the design lines up from one strip to the next.

Match typeHow strips line upEffect on paper
Straight matchThe pattern meets at the same height on every strip.Each strip starts on a repeat boundary, so some paper is lost at the top of each strip.
Half-drop matchEvery other strip starts half a repeat lower, so the design steps down diagonally.Often needs more paper than a straight match, especially with a large repeat, though not in every layout.
Free (random) matchStrips can be hung in any order with no matching.No repeat allowance, so the least waste.

A larger repeat wastes more paper, because up to one repeat can be lost on each strip. The same repeat can need more rolls in a half-drop than in a straight match, as the examples below show. For papers with a repeat up to about 26 cm (10 inches), guides commonly suggest about 10 percent extra, and 15 to 20 percent for larger repeats.

7. Doors and Windows

It seems logical to subtract doors and windows, but the paper does not behave like a simple area. Strips still run across the top of a door and above and below a window, and the pieces must match the strips beside them. Some guides therefore recommend treating a wall with openings as a full wall. Others subtract large openings.

The calculator offers both. By default it hangs paper across openings, which is the safer choice. If you choose Deduct, you enter each opening's width, height, and the height of its bottom edge from the floor. It then removes the strips that fall completely inside the opening and adds short pieces for the paper above and below it, with one extra repeat allowed for matching on each piece. This is an estimate. If you deduct openings, keep a spare roll in case the short pieces cannot be cut from the offcuts.

9. Why Square Footage Can Mislead

Many tools divide the wall area by the area of a roll. That is fine for plain paper, but it ignores two things: strips cannot be split, and the pattern repeat wastes paper. In the room above, the walls come to 32.2 square meters. A roll of 53 cm by 10.05 m covers 5.33 square meters, so an area estimate gives about 7 rolls, even with a 10 percent spare. The straight-match paper actually needs 10 to 11. The calculator shows this area-based figure next to the real result so you can see the gap.

A US double roll of 20.5 inches by 33 feet is about 56 square feet on paper, but you will rarely cover that much wall with it, since each strip loses length to trim and pattern. If you need wall area itself, for example for paint, our Square Footage Calculator adds up walls and subtracts openings. For wallpaper, count strips.

10. Buying Tips

  • Buy all your rolls at once and check that they carry the same batch or dye lot number. Colors can differ between batches.
  • Allow more spare for large repeats, half-drop matches, and out-of-square walls. About 10 percent suits small repeats, and 15 to 20 percent suits large ones.
  • Keep a spare roll for repairs, especially if the pattern may be discontinued.
  • Check whether the price is per single roll or per double roll, and whether the length on the label is per roll as sold.
  • If you are unsure, ask your supplier or installer, especially for a complicated room.

11. Common Mistakes

  • Using square footage for patterned paper. It ignores strips and repeat.
  • Adding all the walls together. Each wall rounds up to whole strips on its own.
  • Forgetting trim. Strips must be longer than the wall height.
  • Mixing up repeat and match. The same repeat uses more paper in a half-drop.
  • Using the wrong roll length. A double roll is twice a single roll, and the label tells you which you are buying.
  • Mixing units. Do not enter inches in a field set to centimeters.
  • Deducting every opening. It can leave you short.
  • Ordering the exact number. Add a spare allowance.

12. Related Calculations and Use Cases

The same method works for feature walls, whole rooms, hallways, and stairwells, as long as you enter each wall separately. For a sloped ceiling or a gable end, use the tallest height of each wall, and expect extra waste. Murals and panel designs are sold by the panel, and need their own dimensions, so this calculator does not cover them.
