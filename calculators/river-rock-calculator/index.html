River Rock Calculator: How Much You Need, By Size — From Pea Gravel to Riprap

Why the math changes for bigger rock, and how to get an accurate order the first time

📌 Quick Answer

For small-to-medium river rock: Weight = Length(ft) × Width(ft) × Depth(ft) × Density (standard river rock ≈ 89 lb/cu ft, or ~1.2 tons/cu yd). A 6×4 ft bed at 10 inches deep needs about 1,780 lbs (0.89 tons). For large rock (3"+) and riprap, skip the depth math entirely — it's placed as a single layer, so use a coverage rate instead: roughly 50 sq ft per ton for 3-6" rock, or 25 sq ft per ton for 6"+ rock. Order too little river rock, and you're back at the supplier for a second delivery — often with another delivery fee attached. Order too much, and you've paid for stone sitting in a pile in your driveway. Getting the number right the first time matters, and most calculators only give you half the picture.

This river rock calculator does something most others skip: it changes its own formula depending on the rock size you select. That's not a gimmick — it's how the material actually behaves.

1. Why Rock Size Changes the Math

Small stone (pea gravel through medium river rock) settles and fills a measured depth fairly predictably — you can calculate a volume, multiply by density, and get a reliable weight. Large river rock and riprap don't work that way. Big stones are placed as a single layer, with irregular gaps between them, so calculating "volume × depth" for a 5-inch stone doesn't reflect how it's actually installed or sold. That's why professional suppliers quote large rock in coverage area per ton instead of a depth-based volume — this single-layer placement approach is also how riprap is specified in official engineering guidance, such as the USDA Natural Resources Conservation Service's Rock Riprap construction specification — and why this calculator automatically switches models when you pick a larger rock size.

2. The Volume Formula (Pea Gravel to Medium Rock)

Weight (lbs) = Length (ft) × Width (ft) × Depth (ft) × Density (lb/cu ft)

Divide by 2,000 for tons, or divide the volume (before multiplying by density) by 27 for cubic yards.

Worked example: A 6 ft × 4 ft bed, 10 inches (0.833 ft) deep, filled with standard river rock (89 lb/cu ft):

Area = 6 × 4 = 24 sq ft. Volume = 24 × 0.833 = 20 cu ft. Weight = 20 × 89 = 1,780 lbs (0.89 tons). Cubic yards = 20 ÷ 27 ≈ 0.74 cu yd.

3. The Coverage Formula (Large Rock & Riprap)

Tons = Area (sq ft) ÷ Coverage Rate (sq ft per ton)

Typical coverage: 3-6" rock ≈ 50 sq ft/ton (single layer). 6"+ rock or riprap ≈ 25 sq ft/ton (single layer).

Worked example: A 10 ft × 10 ft area (100 sq ft) covered with 3-6" river rock: 100 ÷ 50 = 2 tons. No depth entry needed — the rock itself sets the depth.

5. Calculating Area for Any Bed Shape

Most river rock beds aren't perfect rectangles. Use these standard formulas (all measurements in feet):

  • Rectangular: Length × Width
  • Circular: π × (Diameter ÷ 2)²
  • Triangular: (Base × Height) ÷ 2

The Area Shape Helper tab above calculates this for you and sends the result straight into the Rock Needed tab with one click. For irregular or dry-creek-bed shapes, break the area into a few simple rectangles/circles/triangles, calculate each, and add them together.

6. Buying River Rock: Ton, Yard, or Bag?

  • By the ton: Standard for larger orders (most driveways, large beds) and how big rock/riprap is almost always sold.
  • By the cubic yard: Common for mid-size orders of small-to-medium rock, especially bulk delivery.
  • By the bag: Practical for small projects — typically sold in 0.5 cu ft bags (roughly 40-50 lbs depending on rock type).

7. Common Estimating Mistakes

  • Using one density number for every rock size: a pea-gravel density figure will under-order large rock, and vice versa — see the size chart above.
  • Applying depth math to large rock or riprap: big stone is a single layer, not a "3-inch-deep fill" — use the coverage formula instead.
  • Skipping the waste allowance: uneven ground, edge overfill, and settling typically eat up 5-10% extra material.
  • Forgetting depth conversion: depth is usually specified in inches but the formula needs feet — divide by 12 before multiplying.

9. Frequently Asked Questions

How much river rock do I need?

Multiply length and width in feet to get square footage, multiply by desired depth in feet, then multiply by the rock's density to get weight. For example, a 6x4 ft area at 10 inches deep with standard river rock (89 lb/cu ft) needs about 1,780 lbs, or 0.89 tons.

How many square feet does a ton of river rock cover?

It depends on rock size and depth. Small river rock at a standard 2-3 inch depth covers roughly 80-100 sq ft per ton. Larger river rock (3-6 inches), placed as a single layer, covers roughly 50 sq ft per ton, and extra-large rock or riprap (6+ inches) covers roughly 25 sq ft per ton.

How deep should river rock be?

Depth depends on rock size: pea gravel is typically laid 2 inches deep, small to medium river rock 3-4 inches deep. Larger river rock (3 inches and up) is placed as a single layer rather than measured by depth, since the stones themselves are the depth.

How much does a cubic yard of river rock weigh?

Standard river rock weighs roughly 2,400 lbs (1.2 tons) per cubic yard, though this varies by rock size and moisture content — larger rocks have more air gaps between them, which changes the effective weight per volume. Always confirm with your supplier for their specific material.

Is river rock sold by the ton or by the yard?

Most landscape suppliers sell river rock by the ton for larger orders, though smaller quantities are often sold by the cubic yard or in 0.5-cubic-foot bags for small projects.

This calculator provides planning estimates only. Density and coverage rates vary by supplier, moisture content, and exact stone shape — always confirm final quantities with your material supplier before ordering.
